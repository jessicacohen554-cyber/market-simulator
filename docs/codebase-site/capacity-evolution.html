Evolution & Policy

Capacity Evolution

How the fleet changes year over year — a six-step loop of retirements, additions, retrofits, and new builds, driven by economics and policy, repeated from 2026 to 2050.

§ 1

The Six Steps

Every simulated year, the fleet passes through six sequential capacity-evolution steps before the next year's dispatch LP runs. The loop is one-pass — no within-year convergence iteration — and the output of each step feeds the next.

🔻 Steps 1–2: Exits
Known retirements are deterministic (filed closures). Economic retirements screen inframarginal margin against FOM — with per-fuel hurdle thresholds and a reliability floor that prevents thermal capacity from falling below (peak − firm clean) × 1.15.
🏗️ Steps 3–5: Entry
Known additions come from the EIA-860 pipeline. CCS retrofits target gas-CCs with ≥15yr remaining life. Economic new entry compares Wright's-Law LCOE against expected revenue, respecting per-tech annual queue caps.
🛡️ Step 6: Backstop
After market-driven evolution, check the planning reserve margin. If firm capacity is short, force-build gas-CT peakers to close the gap. This ensures reliability even when market signals don't drive enough thermal entry — an adequacy backstop that is reserve_margin_build_enabled (default off).

§ 3

Economic Retirement Logic

The going-forward test: does the unit earn enough to justify staying open? Revenue from energy margins, attribute payments, and capacity markets is weighed against the fixed O&M cost of continued operation.

The going-forward test

For each thermal unit, the model computes the inframarginal energy margin:

# capacity.py — economic retirement screen
margin = Σ (price[t] − mc[g,t]) × dispatch[g,t]
  # summed over all hours t

if margin < FOM × multiplier × pmax
  for N consecutive years:
    retire(g)

The margin is threaded from the prior year's dispatch results as prior_results["mc_cost"] — never gross revenue. A unit retires when its margin falls below FOM for N consecutive years, where N varies by fuel type to reflect different owner tolerance for losses.

⚠ Reliability floor
Even when economics say "retire", the model won't let firm thermal capacity fall below (peak demand − firm clean capacity) × 1.15. This prevents the model from retiring into a reliability crisis.

§ 4

Wright's-Law Learning

Technology costs fall as cumulative deployment grows. The model uses Wright's Law — cost declines as a power function of cumulative capacity — to endogenize CAPEX for solar, wind, and batteries.

Learning Curves: Cost vs Cumulative Deployment

Log-log plot of capital cost ($/kW) vs cumulative global deployment (GW). Li-ion batteries have the steepest learning curve (~21% cost reduction per capacity doubling); solar follows at ~15%. These cost trajectories drive the economic new-entry screening in Step 5.

The formula

# Wright's Law
Cost(t) = C₀ × (Cumulative(t) / Cumulative(0))−α

# where α = −log₂(1 − learning_rate)
# Solar α = 0.238 (15.2% LR)
# Li-Ion α = 0.340 (21.0% LR)

The learning exponent α is derived from the learning rate: if cumulative capacity doubles and cost falls by 15%, the learning rate is 15% and α = −log₂(0.85) ≈ 0.234.

§ 5

Storage New Entry

Storage grows via an economics-based value stack — not a fixed growth rate. Each technology's revenue potential (arbitrage + capacity value + ancillary services) is compared against its annualized LCOE.

Storage Value Stack vs LCOE (2026)

Stacked bars show annual revenue per kW from arbitrage (buy low / sell high), capacity-market payments (ELCC × capacity price), and ancillary services. The red line marks annualized LCOE. Technologies with the bar above the line are economic; below it, they wait for CAPEX decline.
📈 Arbitrage
Revenue from buying during low-price hours and selling during high-price hours. Driven by price spread × round-trip efficiency × power capacity. Longer duration captures more spread.
🏛️ Capacity Value
Payment for availability during peak demand. Calculated as ELCC × net-CONE × saturation derate. ERCOT pays $0 (energy-only); PJM/NYISO/NEISO/CAISO pay via capacity markets. Longer duration → higher ELCC.
⚡ Ancillary Services
Revenue from regulation, spinning reserves, and voltage support. Typically $8–15/kW/yr for standalone batteries. Often absorbed by larger generators in the fleet.

Battery Cycling: 48-Hour SOC Trace

4h Battery SOC 8h Battery SOC LMP ($/MWh)
Two summer peak days (Jul 15–16). The 4-hour battery fully cycles each day — charging overnight when LMP is ~$23/MWh, discharging during the morning ramp when LMP reaches $60–70/MWh, then sitting empty during the afternoon superpeak. The 8-hour battery distributes discharge across a wider window, capturing peak afternoon prices ($85–125/MWh) that the exhausted 4h battery misses.

Duration matters for value capture. The 4h battery cycles daily but misses the highest-price hours because it's already empty. The 8h battery earns more per kW because it can discharge across the full superpeak window. This duration advantage is why the model's value-stack screening tilts toward longer duration at high storage penetration — the STORAGE_TECH_BUILD_SHARE_CAP diversifies the build across technologies.
