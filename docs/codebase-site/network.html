The Network

Zones are copper-plate bubbles; links are pipes with TTC limits. When a pipe fills, prices diverge — and that spread is a signal.

§ 1

Pipe & Bubble

The transmission model is deliberately simple: each zone is a copper-plate bubble where all generators and loads see the same price, and links between zones are pipes with a maximum power transfer capability (TTC).

How it works

Inside a zone the LP energy-balance constraint enforces supply equals demand every hour — there is no internal congestion. Across a link, a flow variable Flow[l,t] moves power from the exporting zone's balance to the importing zone's balance, bounded by:

// LP transmission constraint, per link l
−TTC[l]  ≤  Flow[l,t]  ≤  +TTC[l]    ∀ t = 1…8760

The incidence matrix A ∈ ℝn_zones × n_links wires links into zone balances: entry −1 for the exporting zone, +1 for the importing zone. The LP adds A · Flow[:, t] to each zone's energy balance row.

The shadow price on each zone's energy-balance constraint is the zonal LMP. When a link is uncongested the two LMPs on either end are equal. When it binds, they diverge — the spread equals the dual on the flow constraint.

💡 Dual on the flow constraint
is exactly the LMP spread: $$\lambda_B - \lambda_A = \mu_{\text{link}}$$ where μ is the shadow price on the TTC bound.

§ 3

Interface Limits

Individual link TTCs don't tell the whole story. Some ISOs impose aggregate interface limits — a cap on the combined flow across a group of parallel paths — tighter than the sum of individual limits.

Why aggregate limits exist

Transmission corridors share physical rights-of-way, substations, and stability margins. A single link's rated TTC assumes the others aren't simultaneously maxed out. When multiple paths feed the same import area, their simultaneous flow is limited by the weakest shared element — typically a substation or a voltage constraint — not by their individual rated capacities.

The LP enforces these with an additional constraint:

# build_interface_groups() in transmission.py
Σ Flow[l,t] ≤ interface_cap
  for each l in interface_links

The dual on this constraint contributes to the LMP difference between the import zone and its neighbors — it is part of the congestion component.

⚠ CAISO example
WECC_import → NP15 is rated 4,800 MW and WECC_import → SP15_rest is rated 10,623 MW, but the simultaneous import cap is only 7,500 MW — well below the 15,423 MW naive sum.

§ 4

Congestion & Price Separation

When a transmission link reaches its TTC, the marginal generator serving each zone diverges — and so do the LMPs. The price spread is a real signal: it says "cheap power can't reach expensive demand."

Flow — Congestion: —
📍 When does it happen?
Afternoon summer peaks in Houston drive demand above what local generation can meet. Power tries to flow from cheaper North generators, but the 8 GW TTC is the limiting factor.
💰 What's the spread?
In this example, the North LMP settles at ~$62/MWh (gas-CC marginal) while Houston clears at ~$85/MWh (gas-CT peaker). The $23/MWh spread is the LP dual on the TTC constraint.
🔓 When does it clear?
Once Houston demand eases (evening cooling load) or additional local generation comes online, the flow drops below 8 GW and both zones snap back to the same system price.

Congestion rent = LMP spread × constrained flow × hours = $24/MWh × 8,000 MW × 2 h ≈ $384,000. In real markets this accrues to the ISO and is redistributed through financial transmission rights (FTRs). The model captures the price effect correctly even without explicit FTR settlement.

§ 5

Priced Seams

ISOs don't exist in isolation. CAISO imports from the Western Interconnect; NEISO imports from Hydro-Québec. These cross-border flows are modeled as synthetic generators with price-responsive bid curves — a tranche ladder.

How seams are modeled

A synthetic external zone (e.g. WECC_import) hosts import generators and export sinks. Each import tranche is a LP generator with a variable-cost (VOM) equal to its price band midpoint. The LP decides how much to import at each price — a merit-order of inter-ISO flows.

Separate code paths exist for different seam types:

  • build_import_generators() — generic stepped tranches
  • build_caiso_bidir_intertie() — WECC tie as bidirectional signed flow, repriced hour-by-hour to measured hub LMPs (Malin / Palo Verde)
  • build_reference_price_node() — split import/export tranches priced at the neighbor's forecast price distribution
  • build_export_sinks() — negative-output sinks for exports (LP chooses when to export at each price)

For CAISO, a CARB unspecified-import emission factor is added to tranche VOM — imports from unknown sources carry a carbon cost under the California cap-and-trade program.

🔗 Linked interface paths
The WECC import node connects to both NP15 and SP15_rest with separate TTC limits, subject to the 7.5 GW simultaneous import cap (§ 3). The LP treats each path independently but the interface constraint links them.

NEISO — Hydro-Québec seam

The NEISO model includes a HQ_import node connected to North (900 MW), Boston (2,000 MW), and Connecticut (1,500 MW). Hydro-Québec power is modeled as a low-cost import — hydro generation with a VOM near its opportunity cost. In practice HQ exports vary seasonally and with Quebec's own reservoir levels; the model approximates this via the tranche structure and a reference price node.
