Fleet & Offer Curves

Each power plant in the CAMPD fleet becomes a single LP generator with a rising cost curve — from must-run baseload through economic dispatch to peaking. This page explains the binning system, the merit order, and how coal's take-or-pay contracts shape offer prices.

①

CAMPD Per-Plant Binning

The ERCOT fleet is sourced from CAMPD (EPA's Clean Air Markets Program Data), which provides unit-level CEMS measurements for every facility that burns fuel — actual heat rates, actual emissions, actual generation. In the default ERCOT configuration (use_campd_bins=True), each physical plant becomes exactly one LP generator variable, dispatching on its own measured heat rate rather than a fleet-wide average.

Despite the legacy name "binning," there are no aggregated bins in this mode. Instead, each plant's capacity is split into tranches — must-run, committed, economic, and peaking — that form a rising step-function offer curve. The LP can choose how many MW to take from each tranche, and the optimal dispatch naturally selects the cheapest tranches first. This is structurally equivalent to how real generators submit multi-part bids into the market.

The Merit Order

Sort all generators by marginal cost — cheapest first. Stack them left-to-right by capacity. A vertical demand line divides dispatching from idle capacity. This is the merit order: the fundamental insight that a competitive market dispatches generators exactly in cost-rank sequence, with the most expensive dispatched unit setting the market price for all.

Zero-MC renewables (wind, solar) sit at the leftmost wall. Storage and hydro crowd near zero. Nuclear and coal occupy the flat baseload plateau. Gas-CC units form the economic middle. Gas-CT peakers sit at the steep right edge. The demand line's x-position moves left in low-demand hours (renewables alone suffice) and right during evening peaks (every peaker running). Each step-change in marginal cost creates a kink in the supply curve where a new fuel type enters.

Tranche Offer Curves

Zooming in on a single plant reveals its internal structure. The tranche system creates a rising step-function offer curve for every generator: the first MW dispatched comes from the cheapest tranche (minimum-load committed, partially loaded), and each additional MW requires drawing from progressively more expensive tranches.

For a gas combined-cycle unit, this means the first 480 MW (minimum stable load) bids in at the committed heat rate (6.5 × 0.92 = effective 5.98 MMBtu/MWh). As the unit is pushed further, it climbs a six-step heat-rate ramp from 1.06× to 1.35× of nameplate efficiency. The final 17% of capacity represents duct-fired supplemental power at a dramatically higher marginal cost — this portion only dispatches under scarcity conditions.

In the LP, each tranche is a separate decision variable bounded by its capacity slice. The objective function weights each by its marginal cost. The optimizer naturally dispatches cheaper tranches first within each plant and across plants simultaneously.

Coal Take-or-Pay & PRB Sigmoid

Not all coal is priced the same. The model recognizes three structurally distinct coal supply situations, each requiring different marginal cost treatment:

Mine-mouth lignite (Texas Big Brown, Monticello) sits adjacent to dedicated mines — no rail transportation, dedicated supply contracts. These plants effectively always bear the full fuel cost, and their offer prices are stable regardless of gas prices. The sigmoid midpoint is very low (3.2 $/MMBtu), meaning even at low gas prices, lignite offers at near full cost.

PRB coal (Powder River Basin sub-bituminous) is rail-transported from Wyoming. Many Texas plants hold take-or-pay contracts: they pay for the coal whether or not they burn it. When gas is cheap and the plant is otherwise uneconomic, the fuel cost is already sunk — so the economically rational offer price omits the fuel component. As gas rises, coal becomes economical again and the fuel cost enters the offer. The logistic sigmoid models this continuous transition: at gas below $3.50/MMBtu the fuel fraction is low; above $3.50 it rises sharply to 100%.

Bituminous coal (Appalachian) is purchased on short-term spot contracts with no sunk-cost protection. These plants always offer at full coal spot cost, though their sigmoid midpoint (3.4 $/MMBtu) reflects some degree of dispatch optionality.
