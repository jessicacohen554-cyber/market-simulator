One grid at a time

Pick a grid to see what drives its number: when its emissions actually sit on the margin, and how the cost is built up.

When is the grid dirtiest?

How much CO2 one extra megawatt-hour of demand causes at power plants on this grid, by month and hour. Darker is dirtier. This is why wind and solar can differ on the same grid: each one only earns the hours it actually generates in.

How much does it vary?

Every hour of the year, sorted dirtiest to cleanest. The point is that a single average would hide most of this — the dirtiest hours are worth several times the cleanest ones.

Where the cost comes from

Everything here is dollars per megawatt-hour. Start with what the project costs to build and run, take off the tax credit, adjust for how much this grid's weather actually lets it produce, then subtract what the power sells for. What's left is the extra cost of the clean megawatt-hour — divide that by the CO2 it avoids and you get the headline.

What this covers, and what it doesn't

Worth knowing before you use any number above.

Build costs are national, not local

Every grid is charged the same price for a turbine or a panel, and the same baseline capacity factor. The differences you see come from what the power sells for, how much it produces locally, and what it displaces — not from cheaper hardware in windier places.

Grid connection and transmission aren't in the cost

The cost covers building, financing and running the project. It does not include connecting it, reinforcing the network around it, or the cost of managing it once there's a lot of it on the system. Real projects pay all three.

Imported power is an estimate

When a grid is importing, the emissions happen somewhere else. The default here charges those imports the average emission rate of the grid they come from. That's an approximation: the real answer depends on which specific plant across the border responded. Switch the toggle to see the number without it.

These are backward-looking numbers

They describe grids as the model reproduces them for 2023–2025. They are not a forecast, and they carry no claim about how accurate a forecast built on the same machinery would be.

How it's worked out

Three numbers, one division. Take what a new project costs to deliver a megawatt-hour on that grid, subtract what that megawatt-hour earns when it's sold, and you're left with the extra cost of going clean. Divide by the CO2 that megawatt-hour avoids, and you get the cost per tonne.

cost per tonne = ( cost to deliver a MWh − price that MWh earns ) ÷ CO₂ avoided per MWh

The third number is the one people get wrong. It is not the emission rate of the last power plant running. When demand changes, the whole system re-shuffles — several plants move at once, and imports and storage move with them. What's used here is the actual CO2 response of the entire system to one more megawatt-hour, worked out hour by hour, and then weighted by the hours that particular technology generates in. Solar earns the middle of the day; wind earns the night. That is why the same grid gives two different answers.

With and without credits. The two ends of each bar are the same project under two tax regimes: the production credit for wind, the investment credit for solar. They routinely differ by a factor of three, which is why both are always shown and neither is ever shown alone.
