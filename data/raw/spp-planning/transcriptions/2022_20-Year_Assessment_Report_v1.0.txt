EXECUTIVE SUMMARY 
 
The 20-Year Assessment (20YA) is SPP’s long-range regional transmission planning roadmap that will 
be used to inform shorter-term regional planning processes. There are no Notifications to Construct 
(NTCs) requested for issuance from the 20YA. The goal of the 20YA is to develop a collection of extra 
high voltage (EHV)1 transmission projects for the SPP region to leverage in its integrated transmission 
planning process. The 20YA identifies economic and public policy EHV transmission projects further 
into the future that are needed to provide a grid flexible enough to provide benefits to the region 
across multiple scenarios. The assessment results in the identification of projects that economically 
deliver energy within the SPP region while addressing a reasonable range of future industry 
uncertainty. The resulting library of projects from the 20YA will provide a source of candidate projects 
that will inform the shorter-term ITP assessments for the purpose of injecting longer-term vision into 
those assessments.  
Over 35 months, SPP and its member organizations collaborated on the 2022 20YA. As part of this 
collaboration effort, the MISO Transmission Expansion Plan (MTEP21) Long-Range Transmission 
Planning Tranche 1 projects were included in the models utilized for this 20YA.2 SPP evaluated more 
than 463 solutions. The analysis resulted in a recommendation of 13 new transmission projects,

Four different scenarios, also known as futures, were considered to account for variations in system 
conditions over the 20-year horizon. These futures are briefly described below and further discussed in 
Section 2. 
 Future 1: Reference Case 
o Continuation of industry trends and regulations, retiring older conventional fossil fuel 
generators primarily by age, standard load forecasts, and continued additions of 
renewable resources including wind, solar, and storage. 
 Future 2: Emerging Technologies Case 
o The emerging technologies future is driven by the emergence of key technologies such 
as electric vehicles and distributed generation. Fossil fuel generators are retired earlier, 
and higher additions of solar, wind, and energy storage resources are made compared 
to the reference case. This future anticipates increased investments in cleaner and more 
efficient power sources. 
 Future 3: Accelerated Decarbonization Case 
o The accelerated decarbonization future envisions aggressive energy and environmental 
policy changes. It involves the retirement of all coal and oil generation, driven by 
ambitious emission reduction targets. Federal policies undergo significant changes, 
including carbon cuts and a carbon tax. Other changes to electricity demand and usage 
such as an increase level of energy efficiency from end-use customers and a push 
towards electric heating are also considered. Higher additions of solar, wind, and 
energy storage resources are expected due to policy-driven incentives and 
technological advancements. This future represents a bold approach to achieving 
emission reduction goals and expanding renewable energy sources. 
 Future 4: SPP-MISO Zero Hurdle Rate Case 
o The SPP-MISO Zero Hurdle Rate future focuses on improving market efficiency 
between the SPP and MISO regions. It eliminates hurdle rates, enabling smoother 
market participation and resource coordination. The input modeling assumptions are 
the same as in the accelerated decarbonization future, with the only difference being 
the eliminated hurdle rate.  
Taking into consideration the conventional retirements and the new renewable additions, there is a 
substantial shortfall in the amount of firm capacity needed to meet the increased demand observed in 
this assessment. Conventional generation additions ranged from 19.8 to 23.6 GW within the four 
futures. This is a key indirect outcome of the study, as this assessment is intended to show the 
transmission roadmap. However, a substantial amount of new conventional-like generation will be 
needed in the future based on the criteria observed in this 20YA. Without these resources, the model

1 INTRODUCTION 
1.1 THE 2022 20-YEAR ASSESSMENT 
The SPP 20YA process is designed to facilitate 
transmission investment to meet the long-term economic 
and public policy transmission needs of the region. The 
process is guided by requirements outlined in Attachment 
O to the SPP Open Access Transmission Tariff (SPP OATT), 
the 20-Year Assessment Manual (20YA Manual),3 the 
Integrated Transmission Planning Manual (ITP Manual),4 
and the 2022 20-Year Assessment Scope (20YA Scope).5 
These guidelines provide a framework for conducting the 
assessment and ensure consistency and transparency in 
the process. 
The 20YA process encourages industry stakeholders, 
customers, and other relevant entities, to contribute their 
perspectives and insights during the assessment. The goal is to incorporate diverse viewpoints and 
ensure that the planning process considers a wide range of interests and concerns. 
1.2 REPORT STRUCTURE 
This report provides an overview of the 2022 20YA conducted for the SPP transmission system, 
focusing on the 20-year horizon with a specific emphasis on the year 2042. The assessment involved 
the evaluation of four future market scenarios, known as "futures." 
The Model Development section (section 2) of the report summarizes the inputs used in the modeling 
process and explain the underlying approach and key steps taken during the analysis development. It 
also outlines the fundamental assumptions made throughout the study. 
The Needs Assessment section (section 3) through the Portfolio Development section (section 5) delve 
into the specific results of the assessment. These sections describe the projects identified as having 
merit for consideration and present portfolio recommendations, along with their associated benefits 
and costs. 
When referring to the "SPP footprint" within the study, it generally pertains to the Balancing Authority 
Area defined in the OATT. This area's transmission facilities are under the functional control of the SPP

1.3 STAKEHOLDER COLLABORATION  
In the development of the 20YA, stakeholders played a significant role through their active 
involvement in meetings held from 2020 to 2023. These meetings provided a platform for members, 
liaison members, industry specialists, and consultants to engage in discussions and contribute to the 
formulation of assumptions and procedures for the assessment. 
Various organizational groups within SPP were involved in the process to ensure comprehensive input 
and evaluation. These groups include: 
 Transmission Working Group (TWG) 
o This group focuses on transmission-related matters and contributed expertise and 
insights regarding transmission infrastructure and operations. 
 Economic Studies Working Group (ESWG) 
o The ESWG contributed economic perspectives and analysis to assess the financial 
aspects of the transmission system. 
 Model Development Advisory Group (MDAG) 
o The MDAG provided guidance on modeling methodologies and techniques used in the 
assessment. 
 Markets and Operations Policy Committee (MOPC) 
o The MOPC discussed policy considerations related to market operations and policies 
influencing the transmission system. 
 Strategic Planning Committee (SPC) 
o The SPC oversaw the overall strategic direction and planning of the organization and 
provided guidance on long-term transmission planning. 
 Board of Directors (Board) 
o The Board, as the governing body of SPP, would have had oversight and final decision-
making authority on key matters related to the 20YA. 
Throughout the process, SPP staff acted as facilitators for these working groups and committees, 
ensuring that all perspectives were heard and taken into account. The engagement of stakeholders 
and their valuable feedback played an instrumental role in refining the 20YA, aligning with SPP's 
commitment to inclusivity and collaborative decision-making.

1.4 20YA MITIGATION  
The original timeline for the 20YA of the SPP transmission system aimed to have the SPP Board review 
and approve the assessment during the regular October 2022 Board meeting. However, due to various 
challenges encountered during the study period, the schedule had to be adjusted. 
To address the challenges and ensure a thorough assessment, the timeline was revised, and the new 
completion date was set for July 2023. The adjustments to the schedule were discussed and approved 
during the April 2022 meeting of the Markets and Operations Policy Committee (MOPC). 
Subsequently, a tariff waiver was submitted to FERC in order to extend the deadline, which was 
approved in December 2022. 
1.5 20YA SUCCESSES AND CHALLENGES 
This 20YA, SPP’s first 20-year study since the 2013 ITP20 assessment, presented SPP and its 
stakeholders with numerous challenges and opportunities for success. 
1.5.1 20YA SUCCESSES 
The completion of the 20YA alone should be considered a success. SPP broke barriers in both 
modeling and assessing the transmission system throughout the course of the study, including many 
firsts. 
The following represent the firsts associated with this 20YA. First study to: 
 Model 50 and 60 GW of nameplate wind capacity 
 Model more than 20 GW of solar (Up to 65 GW in Future 3 and Future 4) 
 Include increased peak demand growth rates and energy rates due to electrification 
 Remove the SPP/MISO hurdle rate 
 Model 9+ GW of energy storage resources 
 Retire 100% of SPP’s existing coal and oil fleet 
These firsts for SPP’s stakeholders and System Planning staff, along with the EHV solutions developed 
to accomplish the objective of the study, will provide SPP a path forward as SPP moves into its next 
round of 10-year studies, including the development and implementation of the proposed 
consolidated planning process. 
1.5.2 20YA CHALLENGES AND LIMITATIONS 
The 20YA also had numerous challenges and study limitations. Although the previous section detailed 
the study’s biggest successes, those ‘firsts’ were heavily associated with much of the challenges from 
this study. The scope assumptions themselves were challenging. The amount of renewables and 
conventional retirements alone caused different congestion patterns and new system constraints not 
yet observed in the normal ITP process. Increasing levels of retirements made the congestion more

challenging to solve, increasing run times so much that SPP had to alter its constraint filtering process 
to ensure simulation times were manageable.  
The increased number of energy storage resources also revealed a limitation with the PROMOD 
software dispatch algorithm, which led to energy storage resources adding to congestion or causing 
increased usage of conventional resources. This is counter intuitive to their expected usage and brings 
about some level of uncertainty to the approved base models and project testing results. 
Mid-study the Planning Reserve Margin (PRM) was increased to 15%, but according to the 20YA 
Manual, the previous value of 12% was required to be utilized. This change would have led to the 
need for additional capacity from new generation. The new capacity would have most likely come in 
the form of additional conventional resources like combustion turbines. This increase in conventional 
generation, paired with optimal siting, could have affected congestion patterns on the system. 
Lastly, this was the first 20YA since the Transmission Planning Improvement Task Force (TPITF) 
redesign of the regional planning process. The TPITF's redesign of the regional planning process 
aimed to enhance the efficiency and effectiveness of transmission planning. However, the overlapping

2.1 MODEL ASSUMPTIONS 
2.1.1 FUTURES DEVELOPMENT  
When considering the range of reasonable future market scenarios, stakeholders determined that the 
best option was to utilize the typical ITP reference case and emerging technologies framework, as well 
as include two new futures that explore accelerated decarbonization and reduced hurdle rates 
between SPP and Midcontinent Independent System Operator (MISO). Table 2.1 below details 
adjustments that were made to specific assumptions across futures such as fossil fuel retirements, an 
increase in utility-scale solar, an increase in wind, energy storage resource additions, renewable policy, 
and hurdle rates. 
 FUTURE 1: REFERENCE CASE 
The reference case future is based on the continuation of existing industry trends and environmental 
regulations. In this future scenario, for the specified year 20, coal generators over the age of 56 were 
retired, while gas-fired and oil generators over 50 years old were also phased out. However, 
exceptions to these retirement requirements were allowed based on utility-specific Integrated 
Resource Plans (IRPs) submitted by stakeholders. 
To determine coal prices, long-term industry forecasts were considered. Natural gas prices, on the 
other hand, were determined according to the guidelines outlined in the TP Manual. Further details 
can be found in section 2.3.3. 
In this future, there were additional solar and wind resources compared to the 10-year ITP 
assessments. These additions were driven by various factors, including favorable economics, public 
appeal, and the trends observed in historical renewable installations and Generator Interconnection 
(GI) requests. 
Additionally, battery energy storage resources were incorporated in proportion (20%) to the approved 
solar capacity. This indicates a recognition of the importance of energy storage technologies in 
complementing renewable energy generation and improving grid reliability. 
 FUTURE 2: EMERGING TECHNOLOGIES  
In the emerging technologies future, the transformation of the energy landscape is driven by the 
emergence of key technologies such as electric vehicles (EVs) and distributed generation. The decision 
was made to retire coal generators older than 52 years and gas-fired and oil generators older than 48 
years, with some flexibility granted to generator owners upon approval by the ESWG. 
=====PDFPAGE 18=====
Southwest Power Pool, Inc. 
 
2022 20-Year Assessment Report  17 
 
The future also takes into account advancements in technology that decrease capital costs and 
increase energy conversion efficiency, leading to higher additions of solar, wind, and energy storage 
resources compared to the reference case. 
Furthermore, this future recognizes the potential for state and/or federal policies to promote the 
adoption of these technologies as part of efforts to modernize the grid. The alignment of renewable 
resource potential with company IRP goals was also considered to the extent possible, indicating a 
strategic approach to incorporating renewables into the energy mix. 
Overall, this future scenario anticipates the influence of EVs, distributed generation, renewable energy 
technologies, and supportive policies on the energy sector, leading to retirement of older fossil fuel 
generators and increased investments in cleaner and more efficient sources of power. Additionally, 
Future 2 assumes environmental regulations, as they currently stand, will be maintained leading to no 
changes to industry forecasts for coal and natural gas prices. 
 FUTURE 3: ACCELERATED DECARBONIZATION  
The accelerated decarbonization future envisions a scenario where a new administration implements 
an aggressive energy and environmental policy change. This future is characterized by significant 
transformation to the generation fleet driven by the retirement of all coal oil generation. The main 
objective was to achieve a 93% to 95% reduction in emissions by 2042 compared to 2017 levels. 
The assumption underlying this future is that federal policies will undergo significant changes, 
resulting in mandated carbon cuts and the implementation of a carbon tax. These policy measures are 
aimed at accelerating the transition to a low-carbon economy while simultaneously driving up the cost 
of coal and natural gas generation. Additionally, governmental policies are included that incent a 
change in space heating from natural gas to electric.  
Moreover, this future anticipates significantly higher additions of solar, wind, and energy storage 
resources compared to the previous futures. Peak demand and energy demand increased due to 
switching to electric home heating and increased electric transportation. These increased deployments 
are driven by both the change in environmental policy and advancements in technology that reduce 
capital costs and improve energy conversion efficiency. The combination of favorable policy changes 
and technological progress creates a more conducive environment for the expansion of renewable 
energy sources and energy storage systems. 
Overall, the accelerated decarbonization future presents a scenario where a new administration 
implements bold energy and environmental policies. The retirement of coal generators and oil 
generation, driven by ambitious emission reduction targets, is at the core of this future. Federal policy 
changes, including carbon cuts and a carbon tax, play a crucial role. Higher additions of solar, wind, 
and energy storage resources and increased demand are expected due to both policy-driven 
incentives and technological advancements along with increased levels of electrification due to space 
heating requirements and electric vehicles.

2.4 CONSTRAINT ASSESSMENT 
SPP considers transmission constraints when reliably managing the flow of energy across physical 
bottlenecks on the transmission system in the least-costly manner. Developing these study-specific 
constraints plays a critical part in determining transmission needs, as the constraint assessment 
identifies future bottlenecks and fine-tunes the market economic models. 
SPP conducted an assessment to develop a list of transmission constraints for use in the security-
constrained unit commitment (SCUC) and security-constrained economic dispatch (SCED) analysis for 
all futures. Stakeholder reviewed elements identified by this assessment which limit the incremental 
transfer of power throughout the transmission system, both under system intact and contingency 
situations. The TWG approved the final recommended set of events for inclusion in the event files. SPP 
defined the initial list of constraints leveraging the SPP permanent flowgate list,19 which consists of 
NERC-defined flowgates that are impactful to modeled regions and recent temporary flowgates 
identified by SPP in real time. SPP used MTEP20 constraints to help evaluate and validate constraints 
identified within MISO and other neighboring areas. SPP also considered constraints identified in 
neighboring areas for inclusion as a part of the ITP study constraint list.

3.1 ECONOMIC NEEDS 
SPP determined economic needs based on the congestion score associated with a constraint 
(comprised of a monitored element and a contingent element pair). SPP calculated the congestion 
score by multiplying the number of hours a constraint is congested in the model by the average 
shadow price of that constraint. In the 20YA, a list of constraints was developed for each scenario and 
ranked based upon the congestion score associated with each constraint. 
There were 106 total needs identified across all four futures, 55 of them being unique monitored-
contingent element pairs. The top 25 unique constraints with a congestion score greater than $50,000 
were identified as economic needs within each future. Additional constraints with the same monitored 
element paired with a different contingency were also included if this congestion score was met. 
Additional constraints identified in this manner are labeled as “Repeat” in the tables below. Some 
needs appeared in multiple futures. The trend of larger congestion scores was observed on 
Central/Northeast Oklahoma’s underlying 138kV system. Southwest Missouri needs were prominent in 
future one and two, while Northwest Missouri constraints were observed in futures three and four. 
The economic needs identified are shown in Figure 3.1 through Figure 3.4. They are also listed, along

4.1 ECONOMIC PROJECT SCREENING 
Solutions were evaluated to determine their effectiveness in mitigating transmission congestion in the 
study horizon. A one-year benefit-to-cost (B/C) ratio was calculated for each project based on its 
projected adjusted production cost (APC) savings in each future for the study year. SPP also calculated 
two additional metrics to support project evaluation, the cost per congestion relief and the net benefit 
metric. The cost per congestion relief metric helps assess the efficiency of projects in reducing 
congestion, with lower values being desirable. The net benefit metric considers the APC reduction for 
SPP minus the cost of the solution, providing an indication of the overall value generated by the 
project.  
The annual change in APC for all SPP pricing zones is considered the one-year benefit to the SPP 
region for the study year. The one-year benefit is divided by the one-year cost of the project to 
develop a one-year B/C ratio for each project. The one-year cost, or projected annual transmission 
revenue requirement (ATRR), is calculated using a historical SPP average net plant carrying charge 
(NPCC) multiplied by the project conceptual cost. The NPCC used for this assessment was 16.36%.  
The correlation of congestion in different areas of the system was identified and accounted for during 
the economic screening process. Where appropriate, this included adding new flowgates to screening 
simulations to ensure potential congestion created by projects would be captured, as well as pairing 
certain projects to ensure correlated congestion would be resolved by a more comprehensive solution 
set. These adjustments ensure the projected benefits of projects are not over or understated.

there are no public policy goals or objectives relevant to the energy or infrastructure sector. Public 
policy needs can encompass a wide range of factors, such as renewable energy integration, 
environmental sustainability, grid resilience, and equitable access to energy resources, among others. 
While the focus of the assessment may have been on addressing transmission congestion, it's possible 
that other studies or initiatives are addressing public policy needs separately. Public policy

5.3 PROJECT NARRATIVES  
5.3.1 2ND FORT SMITH 500/345 KV TRANSFORMER 
Less than a mile from the Arkansas-Oklahoma border, the complex system of interconnected 345 kV 
lines spanning most of SPP’s footprint meets the 500 kV system of the Southern and Southeastern 
United States. The interconnection between these points occurs in four different 500/345 kV 
transformers. Since many solutions being proposed within groupings require removing excess 
production from the 345 kV system and dispersing it across the bordering 500 kV system, more 
infrastructure is needed to be able to support new 345 kV lines anchored at Fort Smith. 
Adding a fifth transformer at this location alleviates the burden at the interconnection point between 
the two systems and provides easier access for energy to travel to and from the Fort Smith location. 
Needs analysis and solution development identified one interconnection point as the best for 345 kV 
projects originating in Fort Smith. Adding a duplicate of the existing transformer at this point 
facilitates energy transfer optimally. 
5.3.2 FORT SMITH-THISTLE 345 KV NEW LINE 
In Springfield, Missouri, located in the southwest corner of the state, near the seam with American 
Electric Cooperative, Inc. (AECI), the Springfield-Clay 345 kV Line becomes congested for the loss of 
the Huben-Morgan 345kV Line, located in AECI. This congestion is driven primarily by the McCartney 
Gas Power Station in Greene County, Missouri, directly to the northeast of the Springfield-Clay line. 
While this problem indicates congestion in this area, it also exposes the need for more transmission 
capability in western Missouri, central Kansas, western Arkansas, and northeastern Oklahoma. 
Designed to alleviate congestion by providing more EHV routes for energy, a 345 kV line from Thistle 
to Fort Smith branches a significant transmission gap. This project spans three states and alleviates 
excessive energy on a highly interconnected bus in central Kansas that carries a lot of flow from the 
Great Plains to urban areas to the east (Wichita, Kansas City) and south (Oklahoma City, Tulsa). This 
energy is instead subverted to the Fort Smith transformer and dispersed across the 500 kV system, 
giving it a route that bypasses the rest of the high congestion in the system. 
5.3.3 FLINT CREEK-BUFFALO FLATS 345 KV NEW LINE 
Located to the northeast of the Fort Smith-Thistle 345 kV new line, this line relieves congestion by 
removing additional surplus energy from the west of Arkansas, working in conjunction with Fort 
Smith-Thistle 345 kV line to transmit energy from Eastern parts of SPP’s system to central Kansas, 
skipping over many high-load areas to reach open transmission in the Great Plains. This allows the 
excessive amounts of energy on the surrounding system to be transmitted most effectively to high-
load zones towards the east (Wichita and Kansas City) and south (Oklahoma City). Via the bypass 
established with this project, more power is supplied directly to the 500kV system. This reduces 
congestion on the surrounding 345 kV lines to the high load areas being served, providing paths for

5.3.4  SOONER-FORT SMITH 345 KV NEW LINE  
Another line intended to carry energy to the 500/345 kV interconnect from western parts of SPP’s 
territory, bypassing high-load areas, Sooner-Fort Smith 345 kV aims to address congestion present in 
multiple parts of Oklahoma. The focus is on congestion present in the central to northeast part of the 
state, particularly in load-heavy Tulsa and Oklahoma City. The conveniently located Sooner bus allows 
power to be rerouted over areas of particularly high congestion such as the Tulsa North system to the 
east and Wichita to the north, addressing issues on the Butler-Midian and Weaver-Tallgrass lines. 
5.3.5 HUBEN-LACYGNE 345 KV NEW LINE 
Like the Fort Smith to Thistle line, this project was analyzed due to considerable amounts of 
congestion at the seam with AECI near Springfield, Missouri. In one case, congestion was worsened 
significantly on a 161 kV line from Morgan-Brookline for loss of the Morgan-Brookline 345 kV line, 
both of which are lines interconnecting across the SPP-AECI seam. With portions of western Missouri 
spanning from Kansas City to Jefferson City devoid of multiple 345 kV routes, Huben-LaCygne 345 kV 
was designed as a solution to provide both north-south and west-east flows and provides more 
interconnection between SPP’s system with neighboring AECI.  
Flows on this line relieve congestion not only on lines downstream of the seam at the Huben end, but 
also in the Kansas City metro, and downstream lines extending west into SPP’s system, particularly to 
the Wichita/western Kansas region. 
5.3.6 RIVERSIDE STATION-MATTHEWSON 345 KV NEW LINE  
Spanning a substantial portion of Oklahoma and running from west-east, this project connects 
Riverside Station (southwest of Tulsa) to Matthewson (northwest of Oklahoma City) via a 345 kV line 
designed to address congestion on many needs without identified EHV solutions. This line attempts to 
divert excessive flow from central, rural parts of SPP’s system that interconnect to the SPP system at 
large via multiple 345 kV lines. Like the Fort Smith projects, the energy is instead moved directly east, 
bypassing high load areas with congestion already present. However, this project instead sends the 
energy to another area with identified needs, helping to alleviate some congestion on lines in Tulsa 
North with projected infrastructure that alleviates local needs. 
This project was included in the original portfolios due to its ability to lessen the burden on congested 
elements throughout the entire system as a standalone project with a passing B/C ratio. Benefits from 
the project extend into areas of SPP far downstream from any identified needs, including areas 
southwest of Oklahoma City such as Piedmont and Anadarko that show high congestion.

5.3.7 GRACEMONT-ANADARKO 345 KV NEW LINE; NEW ANADARKO 
345/138 KV TRANSFORMER  
In southwestern Oklahoma, Gracemont interconnects to the Oklahoma City 345 kV system to the 
north and north Texas (and the greater ERCOT system) to the south. Designed to relieve congestion 
for one specific need, the Gracemont – Anadarko 345 kV line provides congestion relief on the nearby 
138 kV system to the East for the loss of the Minco-Gracemont 345 kV line north of the Gracemont 
interconnection point. As the energy flowing to the interconnection point at Gracemont from north 
Texas loses its primary path north via 345 kV transmission, a substantial portion of this flow redirects 
to the 138 kV system beginning with a transformer at Gracemont. The highly connected Anadarko 
interconnection point cannot support this influx of energy with its current 138 kV connection to 
Gracemont. 
This project proposes making a significant upgrade to the infrastructure gapping Gracemont and 
Anadarko by also providing a 345 kV route to Anadarko, where it then interconnects to the 138 kV 
system via a transformer located at the more central Anadarko. The 138 kV system can then more 
effectively transmit the excess energy to the greater system, most notably load-heavy Oklahoma City 
to the immediate northeast, and Fort Smith’s 500 and 345 kV interconnects in the East. 
5.3.8 WHITE SWAN-EAGLE 345 KV NEW LINE 
A west-east project situated in South Dakota, this bridges gaps in the 345 kV transmission from west 
to east, providing more ability for excess energy generated on the Great Plains to the west to extend 
east into a region interconnected to multiple load centers in SPP’s system, including the southern 345 
kV transmission path extending from the interconnection point at Eagle to Sioux Falls, Sioux City, 
Omaha, and Kansas City. Supporting infrastructure includes additional substations and line taps to 
ensure this EHV project with high benefit connects successfully to the greater SPP footprint. 
5.3.9 POTTER COUNTY INTERCHANGE-TOLK STATION 345 KV NEW 
LINE  
Introduced to the preferred portfolio for F3/F4 as a supplementary project, this attempts to relieve the 
high amounts of congestion on the 345/230 kV transformer at the Tuco Interchange for the loss of the 
Tuco-Yoakum 345 kV line. The flow from the 345 kV to the 230 kV system at Tuco becomes congested 
as the north-south flow from this area into southern/central Texas becomes disrupted. The Potter 
County Interchange -Tolk Station line effectively flows in both directions, supplying a path for excess 
energy on the 345 kV system to flow north when necessary or supplying a path for energy to reach 
the system and be routed to the Tuco transformer, or west into New Mexico, as needed.

5.3.11 SIBLEY-NASHUA 345 KV NEW LINE 
To better route power from underutilized lines in the greater Kansas City area, a bypass project such 
as Sibley-Nashua 345 kV proves effective in providing energy to a point tied to multiple high-load 
areas by 345 kV lines. This project's design allows it to inject more energy from the Sibley point to a 
point at Nashua northwest of Hawthorne, allowing more energy to flow southeast to the Hawthorne 
transformers. This project also gives energy a byway to the rest of the system without first flowing 
through the load-heavy zone at Hawthorne, routing it directly to a point that interconnects with 
central Kansas and SPP’s footprint north of Kansas City. 
This project also mitigates the risk of outages on other lines from Sibley to Hawthorne, east of Kansas 
City. By rerouting power, contingencies that show loss of these Eastern lines improve in congestion 
costs. 
5.3.12 SIBLEY-HAWTHORNE 345 KV NEW LINE 
Implemented in F3/F4 scenarios primarily to relieve congestion resulting from an anticipated 
additional transformer at Hawthorne for 345/161 kV conversion, this project is an additional 345 kV 
line running from Sibley to Hawthorne parallel to one that is already in place as additional routing for 
power to and from Kansas City and the greater outlying system. Like the Sibley-Nashua project, this 
line also directs more energy to support infrastructure at load-heavy Hawthorne in the event of 
several outage contingencies in the surrounding area. 
This is consistent with the general portfolio theme of creating more east/west interconnection but also 
serves to mitigate congestion near Hawthorne, one of the areas identified as particularly congested in 
the 20Y model study for Futures 3 and 4.

5.4 ADJUSTED PRODUCTION COST BENEFITS  
Adjusted Production Cost (APC) is a measure of the impact on production cost savings by Locational 
Marginal Prices (LMP), accounting for purchases and sales of energy between each area of the 
transmission grid. APC is determined from using a production cost modeling tool that accounts for 
hourly commitment and dispatch profiles during the simulation year. The calculation, performed on an 
hourly basis, is as follows:  
 
 
Figure 5.7: APC Calculations 
Production costs = Fuel and non-fuel operations & maintenance (O&M) costs of utility-owned or 
cost-of service-contracted generation. 
Revenue from Sales = MW Sold by Zone × Avg. Zonal LMPGeneration-Weighted 
Cost of Purchases = MW Purchased by Zone × Avg. Zonal LMPLoad-Weighted  
APC captures the monetary cost associated with fuel prices, run times, grid congestion, unit operating 
costs, energy purchases, energy sales, and other factors that directly relate to energy production by 
generating resources in the SPP footprint. Additional transmission projects aim to relieve system 
congestion and reduce costs via some combination of a more economical generation dispatch, more 
economical purchases, and optimal revenue from sales. 
Economic projects were proposed based on their potential to mitigate congestion of the top 
constraints and stakeholder recommendations. For each economic project, the APC for the SPP 
footprint was calculated with and without the proposed EHV projects for all 8,760 hours of 2042.

6.1 SENSITIVITY INPUT DATA 
Sensitivity models were developed to assess how versatile the plan is in handling a range of 
uncertainties. SPP created economic sensitivity models to adjust some of the initial assumptions. 
Adjusted assumptions include market wide load demand amounts, Henry Hub gas prices, and 
renewable resource capacity factors. 
Figure 6.1 shows the Henry Hub gas prices for the base case and sensitivities. Adjustments were based 
on the 2023 EIA AEO High and Low Oil and Gas Supply cases.20 The High Supply case is characterized 
by ample supply, therefore reducing natural gas prices. Alternatively, for the Low Supply case, supply 
is limited increasing the cost of natural gas.
