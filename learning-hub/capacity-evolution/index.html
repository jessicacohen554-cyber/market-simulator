Capacity expansion

The fleet you start with

In 2026 the model opens with roughly 750 GW of installed capacity — a balanced mix of gas, coal, nuclear and a first wave of renewables.

Hourly dispatch decides which of these plants run. Capacity expansion decides which ones exist — and that set is never fixed. Each year some plants leave and others arrive.

What leaves

Retirements pull the fleet down

By 2050 nearly all the coal is gone, along with aging gas peakers, an early combined-cycle plant and a nuclear unit reaching its licence end.

That is about 200 GW stepping off the system — each red block a category of capacity that stopped being worth keeping online.

What arrives

New build pushes it back up

Then the additions stack on top: a little flexible gas, but overwhelmingly wind, solar and storage — roughly 740 GW of new capacity.

Renewables are built oversized because their capacity factor is low: you need a lot of nameplate to deliver firm energy.

The 2050 fleet

Same grid, different machine

The total lands near 1,290 GW — a net gain of about 540 GW. But the headline number hides the real story: the composition has been completely rebuilt.

Two questions drive every block in this chart. Why does a plant retire? And why does new build get so cheap? The next two charts answer them.

Why plants retire

Retirement is economic, not scheduled

A plant rarely closes on a fixed date. It closes when it stops earning enough to cover its costs.

As the clean-energy share of the grid rises, cheap zero-fuel generation runs first. Fossil plants get pushed to the back of the stack, run fewer hours, and watch their margins collapse.

The trigger

Drag the clean share

The model encodes this as a sigmoid: retirement pressure stays low, then climbs steeply through a tipping band, then saturates near certainty.

Use the slider to move the clean share. Each plant has its own threshold — old coal goes first, efficient combined-cycle gas holds on the longest. Watch the chips switch off as you cross their tipping points.

Why new build is cheap

Wright's Law

In 1936 Theodore Wright noticed aircraft got cheaper by a fixed percentage every time cumulative production doubled. The same law governs solar panels, wind turbines and batteries.

Plotted on a log axis it is a straight line down: the learning rate is the cost drop per doubling — 24% for solar, 19% for storage, a gentler 10% for wind.

Riding the curve

Twenty-five years of doublings

Follow the solar marker. As cumulative installed capacity grows from 2026 to 2050, the unit cost slides down the learning curve — from over $1.00/W toward $0.30/W.

Each doubling is a fixed discount; the early doublings come fast, because doubling a small number is easy.

The flywheel

Cheap builds cheaper

The loop is self-reinforcing: cheaper modules get deployed more, deployment grows cumulative capacity, and that growth triggers the next cost-cutting doubling.

That flywheel is the engine behind the additions in the very first chart. Retirement economics empty the fleet; Wright's Law decides what fills it back up.
