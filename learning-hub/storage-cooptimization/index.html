The playing field

A week, hour by hour

Here is the only thing a storage operator really trades against: 168 hourly prices, Monday through Sunday.

Every day has the same rough shape — cheap, solar-soaked middays and an expensive evening ramp — but no two days are identical. The weekend sags lower. The spread between a day's cheapest and dearest hour is the prize.

The asset

One four-hour battery

Drop in a battery: 4 MWh of energy, 1 MW of power, starting empty. The lower panel is its state of charge — how full it is, hour by hour.

Its job is arbitrage: charge when power is cheap, discharge when it is dear. The only question is how it decides — and that turns out to matter a great deal.

Strategy one

The greedy battery

The simplest rule has no foresight at all. It sees only the last 12 hours: charge when the current price lands in the cheapest third of that window, discharge when it lands in the dearest third.

It works — the battery cycles, and it books a profit. But it commits to the first cheap hour it sees, not the cheapest one coming, and it sells into the first peak, not the highest.

Strategy two

The LP with perfect foresight

Now hand the same battery a linear program that sees all 168 hours at once. It places every megawatt-hour where it is worth the most, subject to capacity, power and the loss on every cycle.

Watch the schedule shift: charge slides onto the genuinely cheapest hours, discharge waits for the genuine peaks. Same hardware — a deeper, better-timed cycle.

Side by side

Same battery, two brains

Overlay the two. The greedy trace (grey) is jumpy and early; the LP trace (cyan) is patient — it holds charge through a mediocre peak to catch a better one the next evening.

That patience is the whole value of foresight. The LP captures a meaningfully larger weekly profit from exactly the same asset and the same prices.

The catch

Storage is not free

A battery is not a perfect bucket. Charge it, hold it, discharge it, and some energy is gone — lost to heat in the cells and the inverter.

Across a full cycle this fleet keeps about 85% . Draw a full MWh from the grid and only 0.85 MWh ever comes back out. That gap is round-trip loss.

Pricing the loss

The spread has to beat the leak

Round-trip loss is not just lost energy — it is a hurdle rate. To profit, the selling price must clear 1 / 0.85  ≈ 1.18× the buying price, before any profit at all.

A greedy rule ignores this — it trades any cheap-to-dear pair. The LP builds the loss straight into the optimisation and simply skips the spreads too thin to survive it.

Co-optimization

Foresight and physics, together

This is what co-optimizing storage means: a single solve that weighs when to move energy against what it costs to move it — power limits, the 4-MWh ceiling, and the round-trip leak, all at once.

Inside the full dispatch model the battery is just one more decision variable, optimised against all 8,760 hours the same way it was optimised against this single week.
