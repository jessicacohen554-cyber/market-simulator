The 8,760 Problem · Story 01

How a grid clears
a single hour

Strip the model down to its core: one hour, a handful of generators, one number for demand. The dispatch is a linear program — and its answer is almost embarrassingly simple. Use the cheapest power first. Scroll to watch it solve.

Step 1 · The fleet

Meet the generators

The whole market reduces to five machines. Each has a capacity — the most power it can produce — and a marginal cost, the price of generating one more megawatt-hour.

Marginal cost is mostly fuel: a nuclear plant burns cheap, steady fuel; a gas combustion turbine burns expensive gas inefficiently. That single number, $/MWh, is all the linear program needs to know about a plant.

Step 2 · Merit order

Stack them by cost

Sort the fleet from cheapest to most expensive and stack it — the merit order. Nuclear settles at the bottom at $15/MWh; the gas peaker sits on top at $80/MWh.

This staircase is the grid's supply curve. The LP never reorders it — the cheapest megawatt-hour available is always the next one it will buy.

Step 3 · The load

The demand line

Demand is not one number — it breathes across the day. Overnight the grid idles near 200 MW; by mid-afternoon it climbs to a peak around 400 MW.

Lay that 24-hour curve against the same megawatt axis as the stack. Every hour, the LP must find generation that exactly meets the line — the energy-balance constraint.

Step 4 · The solve

The LP fills from cheapest

Now watch it clear. For each hour the program fills the stack from the bottom: nuclear first, then wind, then coal — buying the cheapest megawatt-hour available until supply meets the demand line.

The plant that supplies that last megawatt is the marginal generator. Its cost is the price the whole market pays — and it shifts hour by hour as the demand line sweeps across the day.

Step 5 · The price

The price is the last unit needed

Freeze the solve at the afternoon peak. The stack has filled from the bottom, and one plant sits part-dispatched at the top of the fill — the marginal generator.

The energy-balance constraint carries a dual variable: the cost of serving one more megawatt. That dual is exactly the marginal generator's cost — $60/MWh here. Every megawatt clears at that one price, even the nuclear bought at $15.

Step 6 · Zero-cost wind

Add wind at zero cost

Bring a 150 MW wind farm onto the system. Its fuel is free, so its marginal cost is $0/MWh — it slots in beneath everything else, at the very bottom of the merit order.

Wind output is not steady; it rises and falls with the weather. But every megawatt it produces is a megawatt the grid no longer buys from the top of the stack. Watch the expensive gas get pushed off.

Step 7 · Curtailment

Surplus wind has nowhere to go

Double the wind farm to 300 MW. On breezy overnight hours it can generate more power than the whole grid is asking for.

The energy balance forbids overproduction, so the LP curtails the surplus — wind is available but left unused. With a zero-cost unit on the margin, the dual variable collapses and the price falls to $0.

Step 8 · Why LP wins

Why the LP beats a heuristic

A greedy heuristic clears each hour on its own, cheapest-first. It cannot see that the gas peaker it switches off in the midday valley will be wanted again at the evening peak — and it pays a fresh start-up cost each time it cycles.

The linear program co-optimizes all 24 hours at once. It holds the peaker online through the cheap hours, trading a little extra fuel for one fewer start — and lands on a lower total cost.
