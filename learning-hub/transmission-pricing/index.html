The 8,760 Problem · Story 04

One grid, one price —
until a wire fills up

ERCOT is one synchronous grid, but it is not one big node. Split it into its four zones — North, South, West and Houston — and the wires between them have limits. When a line hits its transfer capability, a single price tears into several. Scroll to watch congestion split the map.

The geography

Four zones, one grid

The dispatch model carves ERCOT into four load zones: North around the Dallas–Fort Worth metroplex, Houston on the Gulf Coast, South toward San Antonio, and a sprawling, lightly-populated West.

Each circle is sized by how much load it carries. North and Houston are the demand heavyweights; West is small — but it is where the wind blows.

The wires

Six links, each with a limit

The zones are tied together by six transfer links. Every link has a total transfer capability — the most power it can carry — drawn here in megawatts.

Note the asymmetry. North–Houston is a fat 8,000 MW corridor. The links out of West are thin: 3,000 to North, 2,500 to Houston, just 2,000 to South. West can only push so much power before those wires run out.

The simple model

If the grid were one node

Start with the easy assumption: a copper plate . Pretend every wire has infinite capacity. Power sloshes wherever it is needed at no cost.

Under copper-plate, there is exactly one price. West's cheap wind reaches every zone, all four nodes glow the same colour, and congestion simply does not exist. Clean — and wrong.

The wire fills up

Congestion splits the price

Now enforce the limits. West is generating far more wind than its own small load can use, so it exports — but its links saturate at a combined 7,500 MW.

The binding links flash red. Stranded behind them, West's surplus wind has nowhere to go: its local price collapses, while North, South and Houston still pay for gas. One price has become two.

Explore it

Drag the West wind

Use the slider to change how hard West is blowing. Below about 12 GW the export links have slack — one price holds across the grid.

Push past that point and the links bind. Every extra gigawatt of trapped wind drives the West price down and widens the spread against the rest of the grid.

Side by side

Copper-plate vs zonal

Same hour, same fleet, same demand — priced two ways. The copper-plate model returns one tidy number for the whole system.

The zonal solve returns four. North, South and Houston sit above the copper-plate price — they are short of cheap imports — while West sits far below it. The wire, not the generator, is setting the difference.

Who collects the gap

Congestion has a rent

That price gap, multiplied by the power flowing across the binding links, is congestion rent — value that belongs to neither the cheap generator nor the expensive load, but to the constrained wire itself.

It is the market's signal, in dollars per hour, that this corridor is worth more copper. Persistent rent is how a grid decides where to build the next transmission line.

The takeaway

Price separation is physics

Zonal prices are not an accounting quirk. They are the linear program reporting, hour by hour, exactly where the network cannot deliver the cheapest available megawatt.

Ease the wind back below the limit and the prices snap together again. Across all 8,760 hours, every zone's price is just the copper-plate price plus the cost of the wires in the way.
